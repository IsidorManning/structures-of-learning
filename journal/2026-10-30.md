I think of the elementary theory of priors in [structural_priors_v0](../field-notes/structural_priors_v0.pdf) as a “hypothesized theory” that I believe abstract useful parts of priors in deep learning. 

While the theory does admit some useful structure, and while many examples of priors can be studied using it, I haven’t established whether it allows me to ask fundamentally new questions, tackle problems from new perspectives, offer new explanations, etc. This is what I will work on November: focus will be less on construction and more on examples, experiments, and connections with literature, seeing how this elementary theory connect to other research. 

I want to see what happens when we force it into contact with reality. What can I understand, compare, construct, or predict using this abstraction that I could not have done as easily without it? I should prioritize my architecture dissection and plan to write some reflection on both my research progress, mistakes, and learnings. I should be ruthless about organizing my research around questions.

I think the priors of architectures, CNNs and Transformers is super interesting as canonical studies. 

Some lessons:
- Organize research around questions, not around specific mathematics
- A contribution is about moving a research conversation forward. A new definition, a useful abstraction, an example, an experiment, a conjecture, a counterexample, illuminating a hidden assumption, all of these move a larger conversation forward. Thanks Emily Riehl!
- A research contribution is not flawless. It doesn’t need to be flawless for it to be useful to another person.
- Prioritize understanding and detach yourself from any egoistic values. If my intent is to construct theory that is useful in understanding our nature, then I need to abandon ideas that do not align with nature, no matter how much effort I’ve put into the them. It is better to abandon ideas that do not align with nature, and tell others about why it didn’t align, then it is to force yourself to continue.
- Abstractions are useful when they allow you to see new things: to ask new questions, to explore something that was previously hard to explore, to solve problems in new ways, to connect different phenomena that were previously thought to be unrelated. 
- Don’t be too quick to expand a theory before you have sufficient external motivation to do so.
